Water Damage Categories and Classes Explained

The first decision a restoration crew makes when they arrive at a water damage job is classifying two things: the water's category (how contaminated it is) and the damage's class (how much the materials have absorbed). These two labels assigned based on IICRC S500 standards determine everything that follows: which materials can be dried in place, which have to be removed, what protective equipment the crew wears, how much equipment is needed, approximately how long drying will take and how the job is documented for your insurance adjuster.

What Are the Three Water Damage Categories?

Water category describes the contamination level of the water not how much water there is or where it came from. Category determines what can be saved and what must come out.

Category 1 Clean water
Water originating from a sanitary source that does not pose a health risk from contact. The water is clean at the time it leaves the source.

Common sources: burst supply pipe, water heater tank failure, ice-maker line break, bathtub or sink overflow from a clean-water faucet, AC condensate overflow, rainwater entering through a fresh storm breach.

What it means for cleanup: most materials drywall, carpet (not pad), hardwood, framing can be dried in place if extraction starts quickly. Carpet pad is almost always removed because it absorbs water against the subfloor and is inexpensive to replace. Category 1 is the least expensive and fastest to resolve.

Important: Category 1 water doesn't stay Category 1 forever. Water sitting for 24 to 48 hours picks up contaminants from carpet, surfaces, soil and biological growth, potentially degrading to Category 2. Water sitting for 72+ hours, especially in warm conditions (Tulsa's summer), can degrade to Category 3. The classification applies at the time of extraction, not at the time of the original leak. Category 2 Gray water
Water containing chemical or biological contaminants that can cause illness if ingested or if there's prolonged skin contact. Not immediately life-threatening, but not clean.

Common sources: washing machine discharge, dishwasher overflow or drain failure, toilet overflow with urine but no feces, sump pump discharge, aquarium water, water from a leaking waterbody.

What it means for cleanup: antimicrobial treatment is required on all affected surfaces. Carpet pad is removed. Heavily saturated porous materials that absorbed gray water for an extended period are often removed because the contaminants penetrate too deeply for surface treatment alone. Crew members use upgraded personal protective equipment.

Category 3 Black water
Water that is grossly contaminated and can cause serious illness or death. Contains pathogenic organisms, sewage, toxic chemicals or other biologically hazardous materials.

Common sources: sewage backup through drains or toilets , river or creek floodwater , toilet overflow with fecal matter, any Category 1 or 2 water that has been sitting long enough to degrade (typically 72+ hours in warm conditions).

What it means for cleanup: all porous materials that contacted Category 3 water must be removed carpet, carpet pad, drywall, insulation, baseboards, upholstered items, paper goods. Non-porous surfaces (framing, concrete, tile, metal) are cleaned with detergent first, then disinfected with EPA-registered products formulated for Category 3 decontamination. Full personal protective equipment is required: Tyvek suits, chemical-resistant gloves, rubber boots and N95/P100 respirators. Containment prevents cross-contamination to unaffected areas.

What Are the Four Water Damage Classes?

While category describes what's in the water, class describes how much the building materials have absorbed which determines how much drying equipment is needed and approximately how long drying will take.

ClassWhat's wetDrying effortTypical timeline
Class 1Part of a room; low-absorption materials (concrete, plywood) with minimal wickingLeast equipment, shortest timeline2-3 days
Class 2Whole room; water has wicked into drywall up to approximately 24 inches above floor; carpet and pad saturatedModerate the most common classification3-5 days
Class 3Water from above (ceiling, attic) or wicked significantly up walls; ceilings, walls, insulation and subfloor all affectedMost equipment for standard materials4-7 days
Class 4Dense, low-permeance materials: hardwood flooring, plaster walls, concrete slabs, masonry, stoneSpecialty drying, longest timeline7-14+ days

Class 4 is the most relevant for Tulsa homeowners because two of the metro's most common materials concrete slabs (found in most post-1960s homes across South Tulsa, Broken Arrow, Owasso, Bixby and Jenks) and plaster walls (found in most pre-1950s homes in Midtown, Brookside, North Tulsa and Cherry Street) are both Class 4 materials. This is why slab leaks and plaster-wall water damage take significantly longer to dry than standard drywall-and-carpet losses.

How Do Category and Class Work Together?

Category and class are independent a loss can be any category at any class. The combination determines the full scope of the job.

Example scenarioCategoryClassWhat typically happens
Supply line burst, one room, caught within hours11-2Mostly dried in place; pad removed; fast resolution
Washer overflow found next morning, carpet and walls wet22Pad removed; antimicrobial treatment; walls dried or flood-cut
Attic pipe burst over several rooms, insulation saturated13Insulation removed; some ceilings removed; extensive drying
Sewage backup in a basement or first floor31-2All porous materials removed; full disinfection; structure dried
Slab leak running for weeks under hardwood14Long, specialty drying (mat systems + slab dehumidification)
River flooding to 18 inches in living areas32-3Everything porous below flood line removed; full disinfection

Why Do Category and Class Matter for Insurance Claims?

Insurance adjusters use the same IICRC S500 category and class terminology when reviewing mitigation claims. A scope that clearly states the water category, the damage class and the corresponding actions backed by moisture readings, photos and drying logs explains to the adjuster exactly why each material was dried in place or removed, why a specific amount of equipment was used for a specific number of days, and why the job cost what it did.

Without category and class documentation, the adjuster is left to interpret the scope without context which leads to questions, delays and underpayment. See

Frequently Asked Questions

Can clean water become black water?

+
Yes. Standing water picks up contaminants from carpet, soil, surfaces and biological growth. In warm conditions which describes most of the year in Tulsa Category 1 water can degrade to Category 2 within 24 to 48 hours and to Category 3 within 72 hours. This degradation is one of the primary reasons fast extraction is critical: catching water while it's still Category 1 keeps the job smaller, faster and less expensive. See

Does Class 4 always mean the material has to be replaced?

+
No Class 4 means the material is dense and slow to release moisture, requiring specialty drying equipment and longer timelines. Hardwood, plaster and concrete are all Class 4 materials that are often saved with the right drying approach. The decision to dry or replace is based on moisture readings, water category and physical condition not the class alone.

How long does each class take to dry?

+
Class 1: typically 2-3 days. Class 2: 3-5 days. Class 3: 4-7 days. Class 4: 7-14+ days. These are ranges actual drying time depends on Tulsa's ambient humidity, the specific materials involved and the water source. Detailed timeline:

Who determines the category and class?

+
The restoration crew determines both during the initial inspection, based on the water source (category) and moisture-meter readings showing how deeply materials have absorbed water (class). These determinations are documented in the moisture map and scope.
