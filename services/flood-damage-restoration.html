How Often Does Tulsa Actually Flood?

Tulsa's flood history is more significant than most residents assume, and the city's current flood infrastructure exists because of catastrophic events that reshaped urban planning.

1984 Memorial Day flood 14 people were killed and thousands of homes were damaged by flash flooding across Tulsa. The disaster led to decades of floodplain buyouts, detention pond construction and stormwater system upgrades that continue today. Tulsa is now considered a national model for flood management but the risk has not been eliminated.

2019 Arkansas River flood in May and June 2019, Keystone Dam releases pushed the Arkansas River to levels not recorded since 1986. Homes in Sand Springs, West Tulsa, Jenks and Bixby that had never flooded before took on water. Levee breaches and overtopping extended the flood zone beyond FEMA's mapped floodplain.

Flash flooding stalled spring storms can drop heavy rain over short periods, overwhelming drainage along Mingo Creek, Joe Creek, Bird Creek and smaller tributaries. Flash flooding can hit neighborhoods well outside the mapped floodplain.

Your risk check your specific address against the current flood map on FEMA's Flood Map Service Center. Even properties outside the mapped Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA) flood FEMA estimates that roughly 25% of flood claims come from moderate- and low-risk zones.

What Types of Flooding Do We Clean Up?

Every type of flooding that reaches a Tulsa home is treated as Category 3 grossly contaminated water regardless of how clear it looks. The cleanup process is the same in principle, but the entry point and damage pattern differ by source.

River flooding
Arkansas River corridor communities Sand Springs, West Tulsa, Jenks, Bixby are most exposed. River floods rise and recede over days or weeks, saturating every material from the floor up to the flood line. The depth of contamination and duration of contact make river floods the most extensive cleanup jobs.

Creek and flash flooding
Mingo Creek, Joe Creek and Bird Creek drainages are the highest-risk areas, but any low-lying neighborhood can flood during a stalled storm. Flash floods carry debris, silt and surface contaminants. Water enters fast and may recede within hours, but the contamination left behind requires the same Category 3 protocols.

Stormwater intrusion
Runoff entering through foundation cracks, window wells, garage doors and basement walls. Common during heavy rain events, especially in older homes with deteriorating waterproofing. Even though it may look like "just rainwater," stormwater picks up soil, fertilizer, pet waste and surface contaminants before it enters your home.

Basement and crawlspace flooding

Why Can't Flooded Drywall and Carpet Be Saved?

This is the most common question homeowners ask after a flood and the answer comes down to what happens inside porous materials when contaminated water soaks them.

Under IICRC S500 standards, floodwater from outside is categorized as Category 3 grossly contaminated. This classification applies regardless of how clear the water appears, because floodwater carries bacteria, viruses, sewage, agricultural chemicals and particulate matter that can't be seen.

When porous materials absorb Category 3 water, contaminants penetrate deep inside the material into the paper facing of drywall, into the fibers of carpet, into fiberglass insulation, into untreated wood. Surface cleaning cannot reach these contaminants. Even HEPA vacuuming and antimicrobial treatment cannot decontaminate a porous material that has absorbed floodwater throughout its thickness.

The standard practice and the only practice that prevents long-term health issues and mold is:

  • Remove all porous materials below the flood line (drywall, insulation, carpet, pad, baseboards)
  • Clean the exposed structure (framing, slab, subfloor)
  • Disinfect with EPA-registered products designed for Category 3 bleach alone is not adequate
  • Dry the structure to measured targets
  • Verify no mold growth before rebuilding

Trying to dry flooded drywall in place is how homes develop mold inside the wall cavity months later mold that's invisible from the finished side and only discovered when someone opens the wall or smells it. See

What Is Our Flood Cleanup Process?

Flood cleanup in a Tulsa home follows a strict sequence designed to protect the crew, document the damage for your claim, remove contamination and dry the structure. Every step is documented with photos and readings.

1. Safe entry
We check structural and electrical hazards before anyone enters. If water is above outlet level or near the electrical panel, the electric company disconnects power before we enter. Gas appliances surrounded by water are shut off at the meter. Structural stability is assessed water-saturated drywall, swollen framing and undermined slabs can all create collapse risks.

2. Documentation before removal
Photos of every affected area, flood-line measurements and initial moisture readings are recorded before any material is removed. Your adjuster will use this documentation to verify the scope of the loss, and removing materials before documenting them can complicate your claim. See

3. Extraction
Submersible pumps remove deep standing water. Truck-mounted extractors then pull residual water from carpet, pad, hard floors and concrete. All crew members wear full protective equipment gloves, boots, Tyvek suits and respirators because Category 3 water is biologically hazardous.

4. Contaminated material removal
Carpet, carpet pad, drywall and insulation below the flood line are cut out and bagged for disposal. In severe floods, this extends to baseboards, trim, cabinetry and any porous material that absorbed floodwater. The flood cut on drywall is typically made 12 to 24 inches above the visible flood line to account for wicking.

5. Cleaning and disinfection
Exposed framing, subfloor, concrete and any remaining non-porous surfaces are cleaned first with a detergent solution to remove soil and biofilm, then treated with EPA-registered disinfectants formulated for Category 3 contamination. This is a two-step process cleaning removes the organic matter, disinfection kills remaining pathogens. Bleach alone fails because it doesn't penetrate biofilm effectively.

6. Structural drying
Dehumidifiers and air movers are placed based on a post-removal moisture map. Daily readings track drying progress at every mapped point. Concrete slabs dry much more slowly than wood framing a concrete slab that absorbed floodwater can take 7 to 14 days to reach dry targets even with commercial equipment. See

7. Mold check and rebuild
Before rebuild begins, every exposed surface is inspected for mold growth. If mold is found which is common when the time between flooding and cleanup exceeds 48 hours our team handles containment and removal within the same project. Once the structure is clean and dry, drywall, insulation, flooring, trim and paint go back in.

Is Flood Damage Covered by Homeowners Insurance?

Understanding the insurance distinction between water damage and flood damage saves Tulsa homeowners significant confusion and frustration during the claims process.

Standard homeowners insurance covers sudden and accidental water damage from internal sources burst pipes, failed water heaters, broken supply lines, appliance leaks. It does not cover rising water from outside the home.

Flood insurance covers damage from rising water that enters from outside river flooding, storm runoff, creek overflow, stormwater through foundations. In the Tulsa area, this requires a separate National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) policy, which carries a 30-day waiting period from purchase before coverage begins. Private flood policies are also available through some carriers with shorter waiting periods.

The gap: Many Tulsa homeowners assume their standard policy covers "flooding" because the word appears in everyday language. It doesn't. If water entered your home from outside even through a window or door during a storm it's typically classified as flood damage and excluded from your standard policy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is floodwater dangerous to touch?

+
Yes. Category 3 floodwater can carry E. coli, sewage bacteria, chemical runoff, agricultural waste and household chemicals picked up from neighboring properties. Wear rubber boots and chemical-resistant gloves at minimum. Keep children and pets completely out of flooded areas. Don't eat food that has contacted floodwater. Don't attempt to clean it with household products they aren't designed for biological decontamination.

How fast does mold grow after a flood in Tulsa?

+
Wet drywall and framing can start growing mold in 24 to 48 hours, and Tulsa's summer humidity accelerates the process. Floodwater is particularly dangerous for mold because it carries mold spores in with it seeding the materials it contacts. After the 2019 Arkansas River flood, mold was found in many homes within days of the water receding. Timeline details:

Can I move back in after flood cleanup?

+
Once contaminated materials are removed, surfaces are disinfected and drying is verified through moisture readings, the home is generally safe to reoccupy before the rebuild is complete. We tell you exactly when that point is reached based on readings, not guesswork. Children, elderly family members and anyone with respiratory conditions should wait until clearance is confirmed.

My basement flooded from rain is that a flood claim?

+
Usually yes. Groundwater or storm runoff entering from outside the home even through a basement wall or floor drain is typically treated as flood damage by insurers and excluded from standard homeowners coverage. A burst pipe in the basement is a standard water damage claim. The distinction depends on the source, not the location. Our explains each scenario.

How do I know if I'm in a Tulsa flood zone?

+
Check your specific address on FEMA's Flood Map Service Center. Properties inside the Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA) Zones A, AE, AH have a 1% or greater chance of flooding in any given year and are required to carry flood insurance if they have a federally backed mortgage. But flooding is not limited to these zones roughly 25% of NFIP claims come from moderate- and low-risk areas.
