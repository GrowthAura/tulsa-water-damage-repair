What Happens to Hardwood Floors When They Get Wet?

Wood is a natural material that absorbs and releases moisture constantly it's always responding to the humidity around it. When a large amount of water contacts a hardwood floor, the boards absorb it unevenly (the bottom absorbs faster than the finished top surface), creating visible damage that progresses through distinct stages.

Damage stageWhat it looks likeCan it usually be saved?
CuppingBoard edges higher than centers the floor feels ridged underfootOften yes, with fast professional drying
CrowningBoard centers higher than edges usually caused by sanding cupped boards before they're drySometimes, after complete drying and acclimation
BucklingBoards lifting off the subfloor entirely visible gaps and raised sectionsUsually replaced once the subfloor is verified dry

Understanding these stages is critical because the homeowner's instinct call a floor sander is exactly the wrong response to cupping. Sanding wet, cupped boards removes the raised edges; when the floor eventually dries and the boards try to flatten, the sanded edges are now lower than the centers, creating a permanently crowned floor.

When Can Water-Damaged Hardwood Be Saved?

Whether a water-damaged hardwood floor can be saved depends on three factors: the water category, how quickly drying starts and whether the boards have physically separated from the subfloor.

Good odds of saving the floor:
Clean water (Category 1) + professional drying started within 24-48 hours + boards still attached to the subfloor. Under these conditions, cupping is often reversible. The boards will flatten as moisture equalizes but only if the drying process is controlled and monitored, not rushed.

Floor likely needs replacement:

  • Gray or black water (dishwasher drain discharge, sewage, floodwater) contamination has been absorbed into the wood grain and can't be cleaned out
  • Buckling boards that have physically separated from the subfloor are structurally compromised
  • Water sitting for more than 48-72 hours without drying moisture has fully saturated both the boards and the subfloor, and mold may have started on the subfloor surface
  • Finish damage (delaminating polyurethane, white haze in the finish) combined with deep saturation

How Do We Dry Hardwood Floors?

Hardwood floor drying is a specialized process that differs significantly from standard structural drying. Standard air movers blow air across surfaces effective for walls and carpet, but they can actually dry the top of a hardwood board faster than the bottom, making cupping worse. Our approach works from below the board, where the moisture concentration is highest.

Step 1 Measure the wood and subfloor
Pin and pinless moisture meters take readings in the hardwood boards, the subfloor beneath them and unaffected wood in another part of the home. The unaffected readings establish the dry target the moisture content the wet boards need to reach. This target is set for Tulsa's ambient conditions, not a national standard, because Oklahoma's normal wood moisture content (typically 8-12%) differs from drier or more humid climates.

Step 2 Floor mat drying
Specialized drying panels are placed directly on the hardwood surface. They create a sealed chamber against the floor and pull air down through the boards and subfloor from below extracting moisture from the bottom of the board where cupping starts. This targeted approach is far more effective than surface airflow and avoids the uneven drying that causes crowning.

Step 3 Dry the room and below
Dehumidifiers control the room-level humidity to accelerate moisture release from the wood surface. Equally important: if the floor is over a crawlspace or basement, that space must be dried simultaneously. Moisture feeding up from below will re-cup the floor even after mat drying has equalized the boards.

Step 4 Daily readings
Moisture readings at every mapped point are taken daily and logged. We track the moisture content of each board and the subfloor beneath it. This data shows the drying curve how fast moisture is leaving the wood and tells us when the floor has reached its target. Typical drying time for hardwood is 5 to 10 days, sometimes longer for thick (3/4-inch) oak or engineered hardwood with plywood substrates.

Step 5 Refinish only when dry
Any sanding or refinishing happens only after verified dry readings confirm the wood has returned to its normal moisture content. Sanding before the wood is dry creates permanent damage that can't be reversed.

Why Do Older Tulsa Homes Need Extra Care for Hardwood Floor Drying?

Tulsa's older neighborhoods Midtown, Maple Ridge, Brookside and Cherry Street have original hardwood floors that require a different approach than modern engineered hardwood.

Original 3/4-inch oak over pier-and-beam crawlspaces
These floors are thick solid wood installed over a subfloor that sits above a crawlspace. The crawlspace humidity in Tulsa's clay soil keeps the bottom of the subfloor perpetually damp so drying the floor above isn't enough. The crawlspace must be dried simultaneously, or moisture continues feeding upward through the subfloor into the hardwood. Ignoring the crawlspace is the most common reason hardwood re-cups after drying.

Plaster walls sitting on the same subfloor
In many older Tulsa homes, plaster walls extend down to and sit on the same subfloor as the hardwood. Water that wet the floor also wet the base of the plaster walls and plaster dries much more slowly than drywall, continuing to release moisture into the floor area for days after the surface looks dry.

Slab homes in South Tulsa, Broken Arrow and Bixby
Modern hardwood or engineered hardwood installed over concrete slabs faces a different risk: a slab leak can wet the wood from below for weeks before cupping becomes visible on the surface. By the time the homeowner notices, the subfloor adhesive may have failed and the wood may be too saturated to save.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I sand my cupped hardwood floors right away?

+
No this is the most important thing to understand about cupped hardwood. Sanding cupped boards removes the raised edges. When the wood eventually dries and flattens, the sanded edges are now lower than the centers, creating a permanently crowned floor that can't be fixed without full replacement. Sand only after moisture readings confirm the wood has returned to its normal moisture content.

How long does hardwood floor drying take?

+
Typically 5 to 10 days with floor mat drying systems and daily monitoring. Thick solid oak and engineered hardwood with plywood substrates take longer. Drying time also depends on whether there's a crawlspace component crawlspaces over clay soil in Tulsa can take additional time to dry. Timeline guide:

Will my cupped hardwood floor go back flat?

+
Often yes if the water source is fixed and professional drying starts within 24 to 48 hours. As the wood dries and the moisture content equalizes between the top and bottom of the board, the cupping reverses. The longer the floor stays wet, the less likely full recovery becomes boards that have been cupped for weeks may retain some deformation even after drying.

Does insurance cover hardwood floor water damage?

+
When the damage comes from a sudden and accidental loss a burst pipe, failed water heater or appliance leak the drying and replacement (if needed) are usually covered under standard Oklahoma homeowners policies. Gradual leaks and slab leaks that went undetected for weeks are sometimes disputed. See:

Can engineered hardwood be dried the same way?

+
Engineered hardwood uses a thin veneer over a plywood or fiberboard substrate. It can often be dried with mat systems, but the substrate absorbs water more readily than solid wood and delamination is a risk. If the veneer separates from the substrate, the floor needs replacement regardless of moisture content.
